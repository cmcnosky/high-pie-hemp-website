Safety first

Read the evidence, not the promise

Straightforward guidance for reading labels, understanding delayed effects, storing products safely, and knowing when to seek help.

Start with the package label Product images and descriptions are not a substitute for the directions and warnings on the item in your hand. Follow that label, and ask a qualified health professional when your health, medication, or tolerance makes the decision uncertain.

Before use

  • Do not use cannabis products before driving, operating machinery or doing anything that depends on unimpaired judgment or coordination.
  • Do not combine cannabis with alcohol or other intoxicating substances. The combined effects can be unpredictable and may increase impairment.
  • Keep every product in its original, clearly labeled, child-resistant package and locked away from children and pets.
  • If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, taking medication or managing a health condition, speak with a qualified health professional before using cannabinoid products.

Delayed edible effects

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention says edible cannabis products may take 30 minutes to two hours to produce effects, and effects can last longer than expected. Product strength and individual response can also be difficult to predict.

That delay creates a common risk: taking more while waiting for the first amount to take effect. Do not treat the absence of immediate effects as evidence that a product is weak. Follow a verified label and do not take additional amounts during the delayed-onset window.

CBD is not risk-free

CBD is often marketed as harmless or as a treatment. The US Food and Drug Administration warns that CBD can cause side effects, interact with medications and carry other risks, including potential liver injury. The FDA also warns that many marketed CBD products have not been evaluated for effectiveness, proper dose or interactions.

A product being described as hemp-derived or non-intoxicating does not make it suitable for every person. Avoid health-treatment claims that are not supported by an FDA-approved use and evidence specific to that product.

Reading a certificate of analysis

A certificate of analysis, or COA, is useful only when it can be tied to the exact item. Before relying on one, check all of the following:

  1. Identity: product name, form, strength and net contents agree with the package in hand.
  2. Batch match: the lot or batch identifier on the package exactly matches the laboratory document.
  3. Source and date: the document identifies the laboratory, client, sample, received date and report date, with a way to verify the report at its source.
  4. Scope: results cover the analytes and contaminants required for the product and market—not merely a headline potency number.
  5. Units and limits: units, detection limits, test methods and any pass/fail standard are clear enough to interpret.

Texas DSHS also publishes current consumable hemp labeling guidance. Requirements can change, and other destinations may apply different rules. A seller should verify current requirements before offering a product.

Safe storage

  • Follow the storage directions printed on the product label.
  • Keep products secured, out of sight and reach of children and pets.
  • Keep edible products separate from ordinary food and never move them into an unmarked container.
  • Do not use a product with damaged packaging, an unreadable label, an unknown batch, unexpected appearance or a missing required seal.

When to get help

If someone may have taken too much, or a child has been exposed, call US Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222. Call 911 for an emergency, including trouble breathing, loss of consciousness, seizure or another severe symptom. Do not drive while impaired; ask a sober person or emergency service for help.

Do not rely on an internet remedy CBD, pepper, food, a cold shower or sleep should not be presented as a substitute for professional help. Give Poison Control or emergency responders the package, strength, estimated amount, time of exposure and the person's symptoms when available.
